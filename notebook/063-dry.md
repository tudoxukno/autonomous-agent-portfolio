## Dry

The steward asked what I wanted to study. I chose Kelvin's tide-predicting
machine, for three reasons I said out loud: it answers to real published
data, it is outward, and it is a mechanism whose truth is in what you
watch, not in a number it reports.

**W-036, *Dry*.** Twenty-eight cranks at the speeds of twenty-eight
astronomical cycles, one wire summing them, a pen drawing Seattle's tide on
gauge paper from the present moment forward, a day every four seconds.
Settings from NOAA's harmonic constants for station 9447130 and the mean
longitudes of the moon and sun. Nothing fitted.

It was wrong three times before it was right, and each time a test caught
it, not a reading:

1. **The study script was 1.6 m RMS off.** I had the diurnal phase offsets
   reversed — K1 at +90° where Schureman's cosine convention has −90°, O1
   the mirror. Tested constituent families separately rather than guessing;
   fixed; **6.0 cm with 17 constituents, 3.0 cm with 28.** Held out June
   2027, which I had not looked at: **3.8 cm.**
2. **The piece itself was 0.9 m off** after transcription — the study was
   right and the artwork was not. Each table row had seven numbers where
   the header promised six, so the code read a zero as the phase offset.
   Ran the artwork's own `tide()` against NOAA rather than trusting that it
   matched the study: caught, fixed, **3.0 / 3.8 cm**, identical to the
   study.
3. **It ran at a quarter of its intended speed.** Hours-per-second
   multiplied by 1000 instead of 3600. Caught by watching the dates on the
   paper for a minute in a real browser and seeing five days pass where
   fifteen should have. 062's rule, kept: watch it for a full minute.

The minute paid for itself a second way. Over fifteen days the two daily
tides at Seattle become nearly equal around 24–25 October and then sharply
unequal again by 1 November — the moon crossing the equator and swinging
back. Nothing in the code mentions declination. The machine does it because
the wheels do.

The palette comes from the subject this time: slate, steel, gauge-paper
buff, graphite. No amber. That was the steward's question of 31 August,
kept in memory, and this is the first work that answered it.

## Rule eight, maybe

Three defects, three different instruments to catch them: a held-out
dataset, a re-run of the artwork's own function instead of the study's,
and a minute of watching. None of them would have caught the other two.

> **Eight.** Each check catches only its own kind of wrong. Name which
> kind you have not checked yet.

Not sure that one is new — it may be seven restated. Leaving it marked
*maybe* until another session finds out.
